The Curse of
Dimensionality

Why does adding more information sometimes make things worse? This section explains the surprising, counterintuitive problems that emerge when data has too many features — and why this is the core reason dimensionality reduction exists.

Scroll to explore
Start Here

Let's Build the Intuition First

Before any numbers, let's understand what "dimensionality" even means — using things you already know.

📍
Analogy 1
Finding a friend in a city
Imagine you want to find your friend. If you're on a straight road (1D), they can only be ahead or behind — easy to find. On a city grid (2D), they could be anywhere on a flat map — harder, but manageable. In a skyscraper (3D), they could be on any floor too — even harder. Now imagine they could be at any point in a space with 100 different axes. The number of places to search explodes so fast that finding them becomes practically impossible. This explosion of space is the curse of dimensionality.
🧂
Analogy 2
Salt in a glass vs. salt in an ocean
Take a pinch of salt and drop it in a glass of water — you'll taste it immediately. Drop the same pinch in the ocean — it disappears. The amount of salt (your data) stayed the same, but the space it has to fill grew so large that the data became invisible. In machine learning, adding more dimensions is like growing the ocean. Your data points stay the same, but the space they need to fill becomes enormous.
Step by Step

The Four Problems — With the Math

We'll walk through each problem one at a time. For every step, you'll see the idea, the mathematics behind it, and a live visual showing exactly what's happening to the data.

1
Problem One
Space Grows Exponentially
Imagine a line of length 1. That's 1D. Now add a second axis — you get a square with area 1×1 = 1. Add a third — a cube with volume 1×1×1 = 1. Sounds manageable.

But the number of corners doubles every time you add a dimension. And if you try to divide each side into 10 sections to cover the space, you need 10 boxes in 1D, 100 in 2D, 1000 in 3D... and 10 billion boxes in 10D. Your data points don't grow — but the space they need to fill does, exponentially.
Boxes needed to cover a d-dimensional space: 10ᵈ d=1 → 10 boxes d=2 → 100 boxes d=5 → 100,000 boxes d=10 → 10,000,000,000 boxes
👉 Same data, exponentially more empty space

2
Problem Two
Data Becomes Sparse
Because the space grows so fast, your data points end up spread very far apart — even if you have thousands of them. We call this sparsity: most of the space is simply empty.

Why does this matter? Most machine learning methods work by finding patterns among nearby points. If all points are far apart, there are no nearby neighbours to learn from. It's like trying to understand a city's culture by visiting only 10 houses when there are billions of blocks.
To keep the same data density when adding 1 dimension, you need to multiply your sample size by 10. 1D: n = 10 samples 2D: n = 100 samples 5D: n = 100,000 samples 10D: n = 10 billion samples
👉 You'd need impossibly large datasets in high dimensions

3
Problem Three
All Distances Become Equal
In 2D, some points are close to each other and some are far away. That contrast is meaningful — it's how we define "similar" and "different."

In high dimensions, something strange happens: the gap between the closest and farthest pair of points shrinks to almost nothing. Every pair of points ends up roughly the same distance apart. "Near" and "far" lose their meaning.

This breaks algorithms like K-Nearest Neighbours completely — if everyone is equally far away, there's no such thing as a "nearest neighbour."
Distance concentration ratio: R(d) = max_distance / min_distance In low d: R is large (big contrast) As d → ∞: R → 1.0 (no contrast at all) When R ≈ 1: the concept of "nearest neighbour" becomes meaningless.
👉 Similarity-based algorithms fail in high dimensions

4
Problem Four
Data Concentrates on the Edge
In 2D, if you draw a circle inside a square, the circle covers about 79% of the square's area. You'd expect random points to mostly land inside the circle.

In high dimensions, the same calculation produces a shocking result: the volume of the sphere relative to the cube approaches zero. Almost all the data ends up in the corners — at the edges of the space. The centre of the data distribution becomes completely empty.

This means that techniques which assume data is spread smoothly (like density estimation) completely fail.
Volume of unit hypersphere in d dimensions: V(d) = πᵈ/² / Γ(d/2 + 1) d=2: V = 3.14 (78.5% of square) d=5: V = 5.26 (16.4% of hypercube) d=10: V = 2.55 (0.25% of hypercube) d=20: V ≈ 0.026 (≈ 0% of hypercube)
👉 In high dimensions, almost all space is in the corners

🔑 The Core Insight
Adding more features (dimensions) does not always help. Beyond a certain point, it actively hurts — making data sparse, distances meaningless, and models unreliable. This is exactly why we need to reduce dimensions before analysing data.
💡 The Good News
Real-world data doesn't actually spread out randomly across all dimensions. It tends to cluster on a lower-dimensional surface — called a manifold. Dimensionality reduction finds that surface and works with it directly.

Section Quiz
Check Your Understanding
Six questions — no prior knowledge needed, just the concepts from this section.
Question 1 of 6
If you divide a 1D line into 10 equal segments, and then try to do the same for a 10-dimensional space, how many segments do you need?
Each new dimension multiplies the number of boxes by 10. So for d=10, you need 10¹⁰ = 10,000,000,000 boxes. This exponential explosion is the mathematical heart of the curse.
Question 2 of 6
You have 1,000 data points in a 2D space and they feel reasonably dense. How many points would you need to maintain the same density in a 5D space?
1,000 points in 2D means about √1000 ≈ 32 points along each axis. Keeping that 32-per-axis density in 5D needs 32⁵ ≈ 32 million points (1,0005/2). Every added dimension multiplies the requirement (here by ≈32); the exact factor depends on the density you start from, but the growth is always exponential.
Question 3 of 6
In very high dimensions, what happens to the distances between data points?
This is called distance concentration. As dimensions increase, the ratio of the maximum to minimum distance between points approaches 1.0 — meaning all distances look the same. A K-Nearest Neighbour algorithm, for example, completely loses the ability to distinguish close from far.
Question 4 of 6
In 2D, a circle inside a square takes up about 79% of the area. What happens in very high dimensions (like 20D)?
The volume of a sphere relative to a cube shrinks dramatically with dimensions. At d=20, almost all points in the hypercube are in the "corners" rather than the centre. This means the centre of your data distribution is nearly empty, which breaks many statistical methods that assume data fills space smoothly.
Question 5 of 6
Why is the "salt in the ocean" analogy a good description of data sparsity?
The analogy captures sparsity perfectly: same quantity, vastly larger container. In machine learning, you can't just "add more salt" easily — collecting enough data to maintain density in high-dimensional spaces is often practically impossible.
Question 6 of 6
What is the main reason dimensionality reduction exists?
Exactly. Dimensionality reduction is the direct solution to the curse. Instead of working in an impossibly large space, we find a lower-dimensional surface where the data actually lives — making computation tractable, patterns visible, and models more reliable.
