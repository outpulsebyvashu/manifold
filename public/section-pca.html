Before the Math

Two Analogies

Understand what PCA actually does in plain language before a single formula appears.

🌅

The Shadow on the Wall

Imagine holding a 3D sculpture and shining a light on it. Different angles cast different shadows — some angles show a tiny unrecognisable blob, others reveal the sculpture's full shape almost perfectly. PCA finds the angle that casts the most informative shadow — the view where your data spreads out the most and looks most distinct.

📸

The Best Camera Angle

You're photographing a long, narrow building. Photographing from the side shows its full length. Photographing it end-on makes it look like a square. PCA automatically finds the "side view" — the perspective that preserves the most information about the shape and spread of your data, no matter how many dimensions you start with.

The Algorithm

Five Steps of PCA

Every step shows where the formula comes from, what it means in plain English, and a live visual that reacts to it.

1
Centre the Data
Before anything else, we shift all the data so that it's centred around the origin — the point (0, 0, ...). Why? Because PCA measures spread from a starting point. If the data is "off to the side" somewhere, we'd be measuring from the wrong place. Centering puts the starting point at the natural heart of the data.
x̃ᵢ = xᵢ − μ
Where does this come from?

The mean of a dataset is the average position of all points:
μ = (1/n) · Σᵢ xᵢ — one average value per feature
We then subtract this mean from every individual point:
x̃ᵢ = xᵢ − μ — this is called a translation
What does this achieve?
After centering, the mean of the new dataset is exactly zero:
μ̃ = (1/n) · Σᵢ x̃ᵢ = 0
The shape and spread of the data cloud is completely unchanged — we've only moved it. But now every direction we measure from here is symmetric around zero, which the covariance formula requires.
💡 Centering changes position, never shape or spread
2
Compute the Covariance Matrix
Now we ask: do the features move together? If one feature is large, does another tend to be large too? This relationship is called covariance. We compute it for every pair of features and pack the results into a matrix — a complete map of how the data's dimensions relate to each other.
C = (1/n) X̃ᵀ X̃
Where does this come from?

The covariance between two features i and j is the average of their products after centering:
cov(i,j) = (1/n) · Σₖ x̃ₖᵢ · x̃ₖⱼ
Writing this for all pairs at once in matrix notation: if X̃ is the matrix with one data point per row, then multiplying X̃ᵀ (d×n) by X̃ (n×d) gives exactly all these products summed together — a d×d matrix.

Reading the result:
Diagonal C[i,i]: variance of feature i (how spread out it is alone)
Off-diagonal C[i,j]: covariance (positive = move together, negative = move oppositely)
Always symmetric: C[i,j] = C[j,i]
💡 The covariance matrix captures the complete "shape" of the data cloud
3
Find the Eigenvectors
The eigenvectors of the covariance matrix are the principal components — the special directions that point along the natural axes of the data cloud. The eigenvector with the largest eigenvalue points in the direction the data spreads out the most.
C v = λ v
Where does this come from?

An eigenvector v of matrix C is a special vector that, when multiplied by C, only scales — it never rotates. The scaling factor is the eigenvalue λ.

Why do we want these?
The covariance matrix C "stretches" space in the directions where data spreads most. Eigenvectors are the directions that don't rotate under this stretching — they're the natural axes of the data's shape.

A crucial property:
Because C is symmetric, all its eigenvectors are perpendicular: PC1 ⊥ PC2 ⊥ PC3...
This gives us a clean new orthogonal coordinate system for the data
Larger λ means that PC direction captures more variance
💡 PC1 (largest λ) is the direction of maximum spread in the data
4
Compute Explained Variance
Each principal component explains a fraction of the total variance in the data. The eigenvalue λₖ tells us how much variance PC k captures. We divide by the total to get a percentage — how much of the dataset's information lives in that direction.
EVR_k = λₖ / Σⱼ λⱼ
Where does this come from?

The total variance in the dataset equals the sum of all eigenvalues:
total variance = Σⱼ λⱼ = trace(C) — sum of all feature variances
The variance captured by just the k-th PC is exactly λₖ. So the fraction it explains is:
EVR_k = λₖ / Σⱼ λⱼ
How to choose k (how many PCs to keep):
Sum EVR values until cumulative total reaches 95% (or your target)
The "elbow" of the scree plot — where eigenvalues level off — is another useful guide
This is the core design decision in PCA: choosing k to balance information vs simplicity
💡 If PC1+PC2 explain 95% variance, you can safely discard all other dimensions
5
Project the Data
Finally, we project every data point onto the top-k principal components. This is a matrix multiplication: each point is "rephrased" in terms of how much of it lies along PC1, PC2, and so on. The result is a lower-dimensional dataset that keeps as much of the original information as possible.
Z = X̃ W
Where does this come from?

Let W be a matrix whose columns are the top-k eigenvectors.

For a single point x̃, its coordinate along PC1 is the dot product:
z₁ = x̃ · v₁ — the "shadow length" of x̃ onto the PC1 direction
For all k components at once: z = x̃ᵀ W (one row)
For all n points at once: Z = X̃ W — where X̃ is n×d and W is d×k, giving Z of shape n×k

To reconstruct (approximately):
X̂ = Z Wᵀ + μ
Not perfect (loses discarded components) but keeps most information
The information loss = the sum of eigenvalues of discarded PCs
💡 The projection is lossless within the chosen subspace — and fully reversible

Key Properties of PCA

✓ Strengths

  • Fast and computationally efficient
  • Removes correlated features (multicollinearity)
  • Each PC has a clear explained-variance value
  • Reconstruction is possible within the subspace
  • Deterministic: same data always gives same result

✗ Limitations

  • Only captures linear structure
  • Sensitive to scale — standardise features first
  • PCs are hard to interpret in terms of original features
  • Assumes Gaussian-like distributions
  • Outliers can heavily distort results

⏰ When to Use PCA

  • Feature preprocessing before training ML models
  • Visualising high-dimensional data in 2D or 3D
  • Compressing data storage or transmission
  • Removing noise from measurements
  • When features are highly correlated

🌍 Real-World Uses

  • Face recognition (Eigenfaces method)
  • Genomics: finding genetic population structure
  • Finance: extracting risk factors from returns
  • Image compression
  • NLP: document topic modelling (LSA)
