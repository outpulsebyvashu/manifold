Section 03C — Techniques
Non-linear Topology-based Faster than t-SNE Often keeps more global structure

UMAP

Uniform Manifold Approximation & Projection. Built on the mathematics of topology — it models the shape of your data as a fuzzy graph, then finds the best 2D graph to match it.

Scroll to explore ↓
Before the Math

Two Analogies

UMAP thinks about data differently from both PCA and t-SNE. Understand the core idea first.

🌎

The Traveller's Map

You have a map of the world, but you need to fit it on a postcard. You can't preserve every exact distance — but you want to keep the neighbourhood relationships intact. Countries that share borders should still be adjacent. UMAP does exactly this for high-dimensional data: it builds a graph of who is connected to whom, then draws that graph in 2D as faithfully as possible.

🧳

Unrolling a Crumpled Sheet

Imagine a sheet of paper crumpled into a ball and stuffed in a box — that's your high-dimensional data living on a curved surface (a manifold). UMAP assumes the data lives on such a surface and tries to unfold and flatten it while keeping nearby points nearby. Unlike t-SNE, UMAP also cares about preserving the rough global shape of the unrolled sheet, not just the local crinkles.

UMAP vs t-SNE: Both are non-linear and preserve local structure. UMAP is significantly faster, often better at preserving global structure (the relative positions of clusters) — though much of that advantage comes from its spectral initialisation, not the loss itself, and can project new unseen data points. t-SNE produces slightly crisper local clusters. UMAP is now the default choice for most modern visualisation tasks.
The Algorithm

Five Steps of UMAP

From k-nearest neighbours to the final layout — every step explained with its full derivation.

1
Build the k-Nearest Neighbour Graph
For every point, find its k nearest neighbours in the high-dimensional space. Then assign a membership strength to each edge: how strongly are two points connected? This strength is computed using a Gaussian-like function, but critically, it is normalised per-point using the distance to the nearest neighbour. This makes UMAP robust to varying density — every point sees itself as being at the same local scale.
whi(xi, xj) = exp(-(d(xi,xj) - rhoi) / sigmai)
Where does this come from?

UMAP starts from Riemannian geometry — the assumption that the data lies on a smooth manifold with a locally uniform metric. The key step is normalising by the distance to the nearest neighbour rho_i:
rho_i = dist(x_i, nearest neighbour of x_i)
By subtracting rho_i before the exponential, we force the weight to the nearest neighbour to always be exactly 1. This makes every point's local neighbourhood look the same scale regardless of how dense or sparse that region is:
w = exp(-(d - rho_i) / sigma_i)
The bandwidth sigma_i is chosen so that the sum of weights from point i equals log_2(k) — found by binary search, similar to t-SNE's perplexity. This is the fuzzy simplicial set construction from topological data analysis.
💡 Every point sees itself at the centre of a unit ball — density is normalised away
2
Symmetrise — Build the Fuzzy Union
The edge weights from Step 1 are directed: the weight from i to j may differ from j to i (since rho and sigma differ per point). To get an undirected graph, UMAP combines them using the fuzzy union formula from fuzzy set theory. This is different from t-SNE's simple average — it gives higher weight to the maximum of the two directions.
w(i,j) = whi(i,j) + whi(j,i) - whi(i,j) * whi(j,i)
Where does this come from?

In fuzzy set theory, the fuzzy union of two membership values A and B is defined as:
A union B = A + B - A*B
This comes from the probability rule for "at least one of A or B occurs":
P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A and B)
When both weights are high (both points strongly consider each other neighbours), the union is close to 1. When only one direction is strong, the union is close to that value. When both are low, the product A*B is negligible and the result approximates simple addition.

Compare to t-SNE: (p_{j|i} + p_{i|j})/2n — a simple average. UMAP's fuzzy union is probabilistically principled and avoids the divide-by-n normalisation, keeping weights in [0,1].
💡 Fuzzy union: if either point considers the other a neighbour, the edge is kept
3
Initialise the Low-Dimensional Layout
UMAP needs a starting configuration for the 2D points before optimisation begins. A good starting point dramatically speeds up convergence and improves the quality of the final layout. UMAP uses spectral initialisation — computing the eigenvectors of the graph Laplacian of the high-D fuzzy graph. This gives a smooth, globally-aware starting layout that already captures rough cluster structure.
L = D - W       L v = lambda v
Y_init = [v_2, v_3]
Where does this come from?

The graph Laplacian L is defined as:
L = D - W where D[i,i] = sum_j W[i,j] (degree matrix)
The eigenvectors of L with the smallest non-zero eigenvalues carry the smoothest global information about the graph's connectivity structure. Specifically:
Eigenvector 1: constant (all zeros in Laplacian embedding — discarded)
Eigenvector 2 (v_2): splits graph into two halves — becomes x-axis
Eigenvector 3 (v_3): second-finest split — becomes y-axis
This is called spectral embedding or Laplacian eigenmaps. It's like doing PCA on the graph connectivity rather than on raw features — giving a global layout-aware initialisation before SGD begins. In practice, random initialisation also works but converges more slowly.
💡 Spectral init gives UMAP a global head-start before local refinement
4
Define Low-Dimensional Similarities
In the 2D output space, UMAP defines a different similarity function from t-SNE. It uses a curve with two learnable parameters: a and b that control how quickly similarity drops off with distance. With min_dist=0.1 the fitted values are a≈1.577, b≈0.895; together they make the curve approximate a smooth step function — keeping close things very close and pushing far things apart more aggressively than t-SNE.
v(yi, yj) = 1 / (1 + a * ||yi-yj||^(2b))
Where does this come from?

UMAP wants the low-D similarity to also approximate a fuzzy membership function (values in [0,1]). The chosen family of curves is a smooth approximation to the step function:
If d ≤ min_dist: similarity = 1 (points are "in the same place")
If d > min_dist: similarity decays smoothly toward 0
The parameters a and b are fit via least-squares to match this piecewise curve at the chosen min_dist value. With min_dist=0.1: a≈1.577, b≈0.895 (min_dist=0.001 gives a≈1.929, b≈0.792). The explorer refits them whenever you move the min_dist slider.

Compare to t-SNE: q_{ij} = 1/(1+||yi-yj||^2) — a fixed Cauchy distribution with no learnable shape. UMAP's b<1 exponent gives heavier tails than t-SNE, allowing more global structure to survive.
💡 min_dist controls how tightly points pack together — lower = tighter clusters
5
Optimise via Stochastic Gradient Descent
UMAP minimises the cross-entropy between the high-D fuzzy graph and the low-D fuzzy graph using stochastic gradient descent with negative sampling. This is much faster than t-SNE's full gradient: instead of computing the repulsion from all n points, UMAP samples a small number of random "negative" examples per iteration. This brings the complexity from O(n²) to nearly O(n).
CE(W_hi, W_lo) = sum_edge [w_hi * log(v/(v+eps)) + (1-w_hi) * log(1 - v/(v+eps))]
Where does this come from?

Binary cross-entropy is the standard loss for comparing two probability values between 0 and 1:
CE = -[w * log(v) + (1-w) * log(1-v)]
Here w = w_hi is the target (high-D weight) and v is the prediction (low-D similarity). It penalises:
Strongly connected pairs (w_hi near 1) being placed far apart in 2D — attractive force
Weakly connected pairs (w_hi near 0) being placed too close — repulsive force
Negative sampling: instead of computing repulsion against all n points, UMAP samples m random non-neighbour points per edge (default m=5). This gives an unbiased stochastic gradient estimate in O(k*m) per point instead of O(n) — a huge speedup that makes UMAP scale to millions of points.
💡 Negative sampling is what makes UMAP 10-100x faster than t-SNE at scale

Key Properties of UMAP

✓ Strengths

  • Preserves local structure well and often more global layout than t-SNE (largely thanks to spectral init)
  • Much faster than t-SNE (nearly linear time)
  • Can project new unseen data points
  • Works in any output dimension (2D, 3D, 10D...)
  • Theoretically grounded in topology
  • Handles very large datasets (millions of points)

✗ Limitations

  • Still non-deterministic (random seed matters)
  • Global structure preserved better than t-SNE but still imperfect
  • Cluster sizes not meaningful (like t-SNE)
  • Sensitive to n_neighbors and min_dist
  • Harder to interpret than PCA

⌛ When to Use UMAP

  • Large-scale visualisation (100k+ points)
  • When global structure matters (e.g. trajectory analysis)
  • Preprocessing before clustering algorithms
  • When you need to project new data later
  • General default choice over t-SNE for new projects

🌎 Real-World Uses

  • Single-cell genomics (now standard tool)
  • Drug discovery (molecular fingerprint visualisation)
  • NLP embedding space exploration
  • Cybersecurity (network traffic anomaly detection)
  • Image dataset exploration at scale
PCA

Linear only. Fastest. Interpretable components. Global distances preserved. No cluster separation if non-linear.

t-SNE

Non-linear. Slow at scale. Crisp local clusters. Global positions not meaningful. Cannot project new points.

UMAP

Non-linear. Fast. Good local structure, often better global layout than t-SNE. Can project new points. Now the standard choice for visualisation.
