What is
Dimensionality Reduction?

Imagine trying to describe a person using a hundred measurements. Most of those measurements carry the same information. Dimensionality reduction is the process of finding the few measurements that actually matter.

Let's Start With an Analogy

Before any equations or algorithms, let's understand the idea through something familiar.

🗺️
Everyday Analogy
A Map is Dimensionality Reduction
The real world is three-dimensional — buildings have height, cities have depth, terrain has elevation. But a map squashes all of that into a flat 2D piece of paper. You lose some information (like building height), but you gain something valuable: you can now see patterns, routes, and relationships that were impossible to see in 3D. That's exactly what dimensionality reduction does with data.
📸
Another Example
A Photograph is Dimensionality Reduction
When you take a photo of a room, the camera collapses a 3D space into a 2D image. Some depth information is lost — but the photo still captures the essence of what was there. In data science, we do the same thing: we take high-dimensional data and compress it into fewer dimensions while preserving the most important structure.
The Core Idea

What Does It Actually Do?

Data often has many features (columns). Dimensionality reduction takes that data and creates a new, smaller set of features that still captures the same essential information. Here's how to think about it step by step.

1
You start with high-dimensional data
Your dataset has many features — maybe 50, maybe 10,000. Each feature is one "dimension." A dataset of hospital patients might have: age, weight, height, blood pressure, heart rate, cholesterol, glucose, 20 lab test results... that's 30+ dimensions just to describe one patient.
🏥 Example: 30 measurements per patient
2
Many features carry the same information
Height and weight are correlated — tall people tend to weigh more. Systolic and diastolic blood pressure move together. When two features carry the same information, one of them is redundant. High-dimensional datasets are full of this redundancy.
📊 Height & weight are redundant — one captures the other
3
We find a smaller set of "combined" features
Instead of keeping all 30 measurements, we combine them into 3 or 4 new "summary" features that capture most of the variation. These new features aren't the original ones — they're mathematical combinations of them, designed to be as informative as possible.
✨ e.g. 30 features → 3 summary features, ideally keeping most (say ~90%) of the variation
4
Now we can see, understand, and use the data
With 2 or 3 dimensions, we can plot the data and actually look at it. Patients with similar health profiles appear close together. Outliers become visible. Patterns emerge. Models trained on this data are often faster, and sometimes more accurate.
📍 Similar patients cluster together on a 2D plot

Why Bother?

Four Reasons We Use It

Dimensionality reduction isn't just a technical trick — it solves real problems.

👁️
To See the Data
You can't plot 50-dimensional data. But reduce it to 2D, and suddenly you can see clusters, outliers, and patterns with your own eyes.
🧹
To Remove Noise
Not every feature is useful. Some are just noise — random variation that confuses the analysis. Reduction tends to keep the dominant variation and can suppress noise — but it can also throw away small-but-useful signal, so it is not guaranteed.
⚡
To Make Computation Faster
Fewer dimensions means faster algorithms, less storage, and quicker results. Critical when working with medical images, genetic data, or large surveys.
🎯
To Improve Predictions
Machine learning models often perform better on reduced data because they're not distracted by redundant or noisy features. Less truly can be more.
Two Families

Linear vs Non-Linear Methods

All dimensionality reduction techniques fall into one of two families. Think of it like two different strategies for folding a map.

Linear Methods
The "Straight Fold" Strategy
These methods find the best flat, straight projection of your data. Imagine folding a piece of paper along a straight line — you can't capture curves. They are fast, simple, and easy to interpret, and work very well when the data has a roughly straight structure.
PCA LDA Factor Analysis
Non-Linear Methods
The "Curved Fold" Strategy
These methods can follow curves and bends in the data — they unfold complex shapes. Like peeling an orange and flattening the skin — only possible with flexibility. They are more powerful for complex data, but slower and harder to explain mathematically.
t-SNE UMAP Isomap
Key Idea

Data Has Hidden Structure

Here's the reason dimensionality reduction works at all: real data, even when it exists in many dimensions, tends to have a much simpler underlying structure. Scientists call this the Manifold Hypothesis. Click the examples below to see it.

What's Coming Next

The Techniques We'll Cover

Each technique has its own strengths. We'll explore all of them in detail in the following sections.

PCA
Linear
Finds the directions in which data varies the most. The most widely used technique — fast and interpretable.
Best for: general-purpose reduction
t-SNE
Non-Linear
Specialised for visualisation. Groups similar points together on a 2D plot — revealing clusters beautifully.
Best for: visualising clusters
UMAP
Non-Linear
Similar to t-SNE but faster, and often better at preserving global structure (largely thanks to its initialisation). A popular go-to for visualisation.
Best for: large datasets
LDA
Linear · Supervised
Uses the known categories (labels) to find dimensions that best separate the groups. More focused than PCA.
Best for: classification tasks
Isomap
Non-Linear · Geodesic
Measures distances along the surface of the data manifold — like driving roads instead of flying as the crow flies — to unfold curved structures PCA cannot.
Best for: curved manifolds & non-linear structure

Check Your Understanding
Five questions — no CS background needed, just the concepts from this section.
Question 1 of 5
In simple terms, what does dimensionality reduction do?
Dimensionality reduction takes a dataset with many features (columns) and creates a smaller set of features that still captures the essential structure — like summarising a long report into key bullet points without losing the meaning.
Question 2 of 5
Why is a map a good analogy for dimensionality reduction?
A map takes the 3D real world and flattens it to 2D — you lose building height, but you gain the ability to see routes, cities, and distances clearly. Dimensionality reduction does the same thing with data: trade some detail for clarity and usability.
Question 3 of 5
What is the main advantage of a linear method like PCA over a non-linear method?
Linear methods like PCA project data onto straight subspaces — this is computationally fast and the results are easy to explain. The trade-off is they can't capture complex, curved structures that non-linear methods handle well.
Question 4 of 5
You have a dataset of 150 flowers, each with 4 measurements. After PCA, you have 2 dimensions. The three species of flower are now clearly separated in a plot. What just happened?
PCA doesn't delete features — it creates new ones that are combinations of the originals. These new features (called Principal Components) are designed to capture as much variation as possible, often revealing the class structure that was hidden in the raw data.
Question 5 of 5
The Manifold Hypothesis says that real-world data, even when it has many dimensions, tends to:
The Manifold Hypothesis is the theoretical foundation for why dimensionality reduction works. A face has 10,000 pixels, but "faces" are controlled by a handful of factors: lighting, angle, expression. The data sits on a low-dimensional surface within that enormous space.
