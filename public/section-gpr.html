The Origin Story

Where Did GPR Come From?

Gaussian Processes were not invented by machine learning researchers. They emerged from a completely different field — and the journey to modern ML took 50 years.

1951
MINING · SOUTH AFRICA

Danie Krige and the Gold Mine Problem

A young South African mining engineer named Danie Krige had a practical problem: given a few drill holes revealing gold concentrations at specific locations underground, how do you estimate the concentration everywhere else — and how confident should you be in that estimate? Krige noticed that nearby locations tend to have similar concentrations, and that this "spatial correlation" could be used to make better estimates than simple averaging. His empirical method became the foundation of the field.

1960s
GEOSTATISTICS · FRANCE

Matheron Formalises "Kriging"

French mathematician Georges Matheron took Krige's empirical observations and built a rigorous mathematical framework around them. He named the technique kriging in Krige's honour. The key insight was to model spatial data as a realisation of a stochastic process — a random function — whose correlation structure could be inferred from the data. Kriging became the standard tool in geology, environmental science, and hydrology.

1978–1995
STATISTICS · BAYESIAN THEORY

O'Hagan and the Bayesian Connection

Statistician Anthony O'Hagan realised that Krige's spatial interpolation was mathematically identical to Bayesian inference over function spaces. If you place a Gaussian prior over functions (saying "I expect smooth functions a priori") and observe data, the posterior is also Gaussian and computable in closed form. This was a profound unification — kriging and Bayesian regression were the same thing seen from different angles.

1996–1998
MACHINE LEARNING · MIT / CAMBRIDGE

Williams & Rasmussen Bring GPR to ML

Christopher Williams and Carl Edward Rasmussen introduced Gaussian Processes to the machine learning community, showing that neural networks with infinitely many hidden units converge to GPs. Their 2006 textbook Gaussian Processes for Machine Learning (freely available online) became the definitive reference. They showed GPR was competitive with neural networks on many tasks, with the crucial advantage of calibrated uncertainty estimates.

Today
MODERN ML · EVERYWHERE

From Robotics to Drug Discovery

GPR is now a cornerstone technique in Bayesian optimisation (used to tune hyperparameters in deep learning, design experiments, and find optimal drug candidates), robotics (safe exploration under uncertainty), climate modelling, astrophysics, and any field where you need not just a prediction but a reliable measure of how confident that prediction is.

Before the Math

Three Ideas You Need First

GPR builds on three concepts. Understand these and the math will make complete sense.

BUILDING BLOCK 01

A Gaussian Distribution

A bell curve over a single number. Instead of saying "the answer is 5," it says "the answer is probably around 5, with this much uncertainty." Defined entirely by a mean (centre) and variance (spread). Two Gaussian variables together form a 2D bell surface.

BUILDING BLOCK 02

A Multivariate Gaussian

A Gaussian over a vector of numbers. Instead of one bell curve, it's a cloud in d-dimensional space, defined by a mean vector and a covariance matrix. The covariance matrix encodes not just how uncertain each variable is, but how they move together.

BUILDING BLOCK 03

A Gaussian Process

A Gaussian over an infinite-dimensional vector — i.e., over a function. Any finite collection of function values has a joint Gaussian distribution. Instead of a mean and covariance for numbers, we have a mean function and a covariance function (kernel). It's a probability distribution whose samples are entire curves.

The Math

Five Steps of GPR

From defining what a GP is to computing predictions with uncertainty — every formula derived from scratch.

1
Define the Prior: f ~ GP(m, k)
We start by asserting that the unknown function f we want to learn is a sample from a Gaussian Process. This is our prior belief about what the function looks like before seeing any data. The GP is fully specified by two things: a mean function m(x) and a covariance (kernel) function k(x, x').
f(x) ~ GP(m(x), k(x, x'))

Any finite set of function values:
[f(x_1), ..., f(x_n)] ~ N(m_vec, K)
What does this actually mean?

A GP is defined by the requirement that any finite collection of function evaluations follows a joint Gaussian distribution. If I pick any n input points x_1, ..., x_n, then:
m_vec[i] = m(x_i) — the mean at each point (usually 0)
K[i,j] = k(x_i, x_j) — the covariance between f(x_i) and f(x_j)
Why Gaussian?
Because Gaussians are closed under conditioning: the posterior after observing data is also Gaussian, giving a closed-form solution. Any other distribution family would require approximate inference (e.g. MCMC).

The mean function m(x): usually set to 0 or a constant. GPR can still model non-zero mean functions through the kernel — the mean function just sets the baseline.
💡 The GP prior encodes our belief about function smoothness before seeing data
2
Choose the Kernel k(x, x')
The kernel function k(x, x') is the heart of GPR. It measures how correlated f(x) and f(x') are — how much knowing the function value at x tells us about the value at x'. The choice of kernel encodes our assumption about the smoothness and structure of the unknown function. The most common kernel is the Radial Basis Function (RBF), also called the Squared Exponential.
k_RBF(x, x') = sigma_f^2 * exp(-(x-x')^2 / (2*l^2))

Parameters: sigma_f (signal scale), l (length-scale)
Where does this come from?

The RBF kernel comes from assuming the function is infinitely differentiable (perfectly smooth). Its form is derived from the Gaussian density applied to the difference (x - x'):
k(x, x') = sigma_f^2 * exp(-(x-x')^2 / (2*l^2))
Reading the parameters:
l = length-scale: how far apart x and x' can be while still being correlated. Large l = smooth, slowly varying function. Small l = wiggly, fast-varying function.
sigma_f^2 = signal variance: the overall amplitude of the function. Large sigma_f = function can take large values.
A valid kernel must produce positive semi-definite covariance matrices K for any set of input points — this ensures the Gaussian distribution is well-defined. The RBF satisfies this by construction (it's a Gram matrix of Gaussian features).
💡 The length-scale l controls how "wiggly" your function prior is
3
Build the Covariance Matrices
Given n training points X and m test points X*, we compute three covariance matrices by applying the kernel to every pair of points. These matrices encode all the correlation information the GP needs: how training points relate to each other, how test points relate to training points, and how test points relate to each other.
K = k(X, X) shape: n x n (train-train)
K* = k(X*, X) shape: m x n (test-train)
K** = k(X*, X*) shape: m x m (test-test)
K_y = K + sigma_n^2 * I (noisy training)
Where does this come from?

The joint distribution of training outputs y and test outputs f* is Gaussian:
[[y], [f*]] ~ N(0, [[K_y, K*^T], [K*, K**]])
K[i,j] = k(x_i, x_j) for all training pairs.
K*[i,j] = k(x*_i, x_j) links test to training points.
K**[i,j] = k(x*_i, x*_j) is the test-test covariance.

The noise term:
y = f(X) + epsilon where epsilon ~ N(0, sigma_n^2 I)
Adding observation noise adds sigma_n^2 * I to the diagonal of K, giving K_y.
This makes K_y invertible and represents our assumption that measurements have independent Gaussian noise.
💡 K_y = K + noise*I is always invertible — ensuring a unique solution
4
Condition on Data — The Posterior
This is the central operation of GPR. We know the joint Gaussian distribution of training outputs y and test outputs f*. By conditioning on the observed y (using the Gaussian conditioning formula), we get the posterior distribution over f* — the updated belief about the function after seeing data. This is where GPR's power lies: the posterior is still Gaussian and computable in closed form.
f* | X, y, X* ~ N(mu*, Sigma*)

mu* = K* (K + sigma_n^2 I)^{-1} y
Sigma* = K** - K* (K + sigma_n^2 I)^{-1} K*^T
Where does this come from?

This uses the Gaussian conditioning lemma: if [a, b] ~ N([mu_a, mu_b], [[A, C], [C^T, B]]), then:
a | b ~ N(mu_a + C B^{-1}(b - mu_b), A - C B^{-1} C^T)
Applying this to our joint distribution with mu=0:
a = f*, b = y, C = K*, B = K_y, A = K**
Posterior mean: mu* = K* K_y^{-1} y
Posterior covariance: Sigma* = K** - K* K_y^{-1} K*^T

Reading the posterior mean:
K* K_y^{-1} is the "weighting matrix" — how much each training point contributes to the prediction at each test point
K* K_y^{-1} y is a weighted interpolation of the training outputs
Reading the posterior variance:
K**: prior uncertainty at test points
K* K_y^{-1} K*^T: uncertainty removed by observing training data
Where training data is dense: large reduction in uncertainty. Where sparse: small reduction.
💡 Uncertainty is automatically small near data and large far from it
5
Make Predictions with Uncertainty
From the posterior, we extract everything we need: the mean prediction at each test point, and the standard deviation (square root of the diagonal of Sigma*) as the uncertainty estimate. The 95% confidence band is mean ± 1.96 * std. This uncertainty is not just a number — it reflects exactly how much the training data constrains the function at each location.
Prediction at x*:
mean: mu*(x*) = k(x*, X) K_y^{-1} y
std: s(x*) = sqrt(k(x*,x*) - k(x*,X) K_y^{-1} k(X,x*))

95% band: mu*(x*) +/- 1.96 * s(x*)
Where does this come from?

At a single test point x*, the posterior is univariate Gaussian N(mu*, sigma*^2) where:
mu* = k(x*, X) K_y^{-1} y — the posterior mean at x*
sigma*^2 = k(x*, x*) - k(x*, X) K_y^{-1} k(X, x*) — the posterior variance
The posterior variance has a beautiful interpretation:
k(x*, x*) = prior variance (maximum possible uncertainty)
k(x*, X) K_y^{-1} k(X, x*) = variance explained by training data
Difference = remaining uncertainty after conditioning
Key property: at training points (with no noise), sigma* = 0. The GP interpolates perfectly. With noise, it smooths through the training points instead.
💡 Uncertainty collapses to zero at noiseless training points — GPR interpolates exactly
Kernel Reference

Three Common Kernels

The kernel is the most important design choice in GPR. It encodes your assumption about the function's shape.

RBF / Squared Exponential

k(x,x') = sf^2 * exp(-(x-x')^2 / (2*l^2))

Infinitely differentiable. Produces very smooth functions. Good default choice when you expect a smooth underlying signal.

Matérn 5/2

k(x,x') = sf^2*(1+sqrt(5)*r/l+5r^2/(3l^2))*exp(-sqrt(5)*r/l)

Twice differentiable. Less smooth than RBF. More realistic for physical processes that are not perfectly smooth. Popular in Bayesian optimisation.

Periodic

k(x,x') = sf^2 * exp(-2*sin^2(pi*|x-x'|/p) / l^2)

Produces periodic functions with period p. Use when you know the signal repeats (daily temperature, seasonal trends, heartbeats).

Key Properties of GPR

✓ Strengths

  • Provides calibrated uncertainty estimates automatically
  • Works extremely well with small datasets
  • Flexible: kernel encodes domain knowledge
  • Exact closed-form posterior (no approximation needed)
  • Naturally handles noisy observations
  • Theoretically principled (Bayesian framework)

✗ Limitations

  • Scales as O(n^3) — slow for large datasets (>10k points)
  • Kernel choice requires domain knowledge or hyperparameter tuning
  • Struggles with high-dimensional inputs without structure
  • Predictions can be over-confident if the kernel is wrong
  • Memory: stores n×n covariance matrix

⌛ When to Use GPR

  • Small to medium datasets (under ~5000 points)
  • When uncertainty quantification is critical
  • Bayesian optimisation (tuning hyperparameters)
  • Scientific data with known physical structure (use matching kernel)
  • Time series with known periodicity

🌎 Real-World Uses

  • Hyperparameter tuning in deep learning (Google Vizier)
  • Drug discovery — optimising molecular properties
  • Geostatistics (original use — interpolating spatial data)
  • Climate science — temperature field reconstruction
  • Robotics — safe exploration under uncertainty
